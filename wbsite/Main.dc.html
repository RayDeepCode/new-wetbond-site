For a hundred years, the answer has been to let water in.

Concrete and masonry are full of tiny channels, and water finds them. So the industry learned to work around it: drain the basement, coat the floor, seal the driveway, and redo it when it fails.

It works until the pump stops, the coating lifts or the sealer wears off. The water never stopped coming through the concrete. It just had somewhere to go.

We asked a different question: what if it never got through at all?

The wall becomes the waterproofing.

Most waterproofing sits on the surface and waits to fail. WetBond is engineered to do the opposite. It follows moisture into the material and reacts with it, forming chemical bonds that make it part of the concrete or masonry. It can’t peel off because it isn’t on anything. It’s in it. Here’s how, in three stages.

Stage 1 of 3

Reactive Penetration

Water finds its way into concrete. So does WetBond.

WetBond is water-reactive. Applied to concrete that's been pre-wetted, it's drawn into the capillaries with the water, follows them in and pushes the water deeper ahead of it. Damp walls are where it does its best work.

In plain terms: it drives in wherever the water is.

Stage 2 of 3

Covalent Bonding

It doesn't stick to the wall. It bonds into it.

Coatings hold on by grip, and grip lets go. As WetBond reacts, it forms a network inside the pores and bonds chemically to the pore walls. There's no surface layer to peel, blister or flake.

In plain terms: it locks onto the concrete itself, so there's nothing to peel off.

Stage 3 of 3

Substrate Conversion

What's left is concrete that stops water itself, whether it's a wall, a floor or a slab.

The treated depth of concrete becomes one solid composite, sealed against water and the moisture that wicks through concrete, while letting vapor pass slowly so nothing is trapped inside.

In plain terms: the wall itself becomes waterproof.

Isn't this just a coating?

No. Coatings sit on the wall, and water pressure eventually pushes them off. WetBond doesn't sit on the wall. It's embedded in it, so there's nothing to push off.

Why coatings fail and WetBond doesn't

And this is our lightest grade

One brick, two halves. One half gets WetBond, the other is left bare. Then we pour water on both, two hours later and again a week later. The bare half drinks it in. The treated half holds it on the surface.

This is WetBond Color, the entry grade in the family.

Bond strength, pull-off test

450 psi

The concrete failed before the bond did. In plain terms: the concrete breaks before WetBond lets go.

Bond type

Covalent

A true chemical bond, not just grip. WetBond reacts with the concrete inside its pores and forms covalent bonds with it, sharing electrons at the molecular level. No film on the surface, so no bond line for water to push against.

Vapor permeance, target

~1.1 perms

Stops water and wicking moisture. Lets vapor pass slowly. A perm measures how much vapor passes through: around 1 means the wall breathes slowly without letting water or damp through.

450 psi against what, exactly?

Water pressure sounds dramatic, but it’s modest: about 0.43 psi per foot of depth. Eight feet of groundwater pushes about 3.5 psi at the base of a wall, whether it’s a soaked yard or a lake. Only depth matters.

Water pressure at the base of an 8 ft wall~3.5 psi
WetBond pull-off test450 psi

A steel disc glued to the treated surface was pulled straight off. The concrete tore at 450 psi before the bond let go, so the bond is at least that strong.

Coatings rarely fail from that push. They fail because vapor and crystallizing minerals keep lifting them from behind. WetBond is embedded 0.7 to 1 inch inside the concrete, so there’s no film to lift. A pull test isn’t a water-pressure rating; that test is under way.
